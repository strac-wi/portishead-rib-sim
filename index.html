      Portishead · Severn Estuary

      RIB: Portishead

      A Highfield Patrol 660, 6.5 m with a Honda BF150, lies on the A pontoon at Portishead Marina, kill cord on. Leave the berth, lock out on the real tide, and work her in the Severn: the second biggest tidal range in the world and a stream to match. Come alongside an empty hammerhead, or practise man overboard.

      Notes

      Portishead in a RIB

      The boat

      A Highfield Patrol 660: 6.5 m over charcoal tubes, 2.5 m beam, a 26° aluminium deep-V hull, a Honda BF150 (150 hp four-stroke, 2.14:1 gearbox, a 14.75 x 16 three-blade stainless propeller), a centre console with a seat ahead of it, four jockey seats in two pairs behind it, a rear bench and an A-frame over the engine. Her hull's resistance is worked back from Powerboat Magazine's test of a 660 (with a Suzuki 175: 38.4 kn at 5,950 rpm, on the plane from about 8 kn); with the Honda she makes about 34 kn at 5,870 rpm with the leg trimmed well, the rpm in the upper half of Honda's 5,000-6,000 band. Her mass (about 1,150 kg with two aboard) and the propeller's figures are estimates. In sway and yaw the hull is worked as strips along her length. Off the plane she sits by the stern and the V slides, so the leg's push swings the stern round briskly (hard over at tick-over she turns in about two of her lengths); on the plane the chines bite and she carves. Take the drive off in a turn and the swing dies within a few seconds. Lying stopped in a breeze, her bow blows off.

      The lock

      The marina is held at about 13.3 m above chart datum, roughly six metres deep. The tide outside runs from about 1 m at low water springs to over 13 m at high water, so the lock works only from three and a half hours before high water to three and a half after at springs, four at neaps. In the window it runs a half-hour cycle: lock-outs on the hour and half hour (the marina-side gates open, you go in, the chamber is let down to the sea), lock-ins a quarter of an hour later (the sea-side gates open, you go in, the chamber fills to the marina). Call on VHF 80 (P) and you are told the next one.

      Signals on the port side of each entrance: three reds, do not enter. Green, white, green: proceed with caution. Go in slowly, come alongside one of the floating pontoons along each wall and make fast (L) to its cleats. Leave when the far gates are open and the light is green-white-green.

      The keeper does not wait for the clock once everyone booked on a locking is in. If you are the only boat, the gates close behind you as soon as you are wholly inside and nearly stopped, and the chamber levels once you are made fast; the far gates then stay open until their next turn on the timetable ends. The gates are never closed on a boat in their swing: wait clear of them.

      Other boats use the lock too: yachts, motor cruisers and the odd fishing boat, on about half the lockings. Lock-outs come up the fairway from their berths and wait by the entrance; lock-ins come in from the estuary and wait along the pier's face. They go in one at a time on green-white-green, make fast from the far end, and leave nearest the gates first. They give way to you, and will ask you on VHF 80 to make room if you are in their way. Bumping one counts as a contact.

      On a spring tide of 12 m or more the marina may free-flow: the marina is let down a little beforehand, both gates open as the tide reaches the marina's level, and stay open while it rises (never above 14 m), five to thirty minutes. Then you can run straight through on green.

      Outside

      Leave the lock along the sea wall and the pier: a channel about 30 m wide is dredged from the approach along their face to beyond the pier head, drying 2.0 m (estimated), so there are three and a half metres even when the lock first opens. Outside it the outer harbour dries 4 to 10 m on the chart: stay in the channel, shown pecked on the chart. The pier head shows a green light (Iso G 2s). Off the pier the stream runs up and down the estuary: east-north-east on the flood, west-south-west on the ebb, at up to four or five knots at springs in the deep water and much less in Portishead Pool, where it eddies westward towards the pier head for most of the flood. Firefly Rocks lie north-east of the pier inside the Firefly buoy; keep close to the buoy. The rates are estimates, not tidal-stream atlas figures.

      Read the stream off the water. The Severn is brown with silt and the whole surface moves: lines of scum and patches of paler water slide past with it, boils well up where it runs hard over the bottom, and it gets rough over the shoals and where the wind blows against it. Each buoy leans and dips downstream, heaps a pillow of water against its upstream side and trails broken water away from it; the longer and whiter the trail, the harder the stream is running.

      The waves you see are the waves she rides: a short swell up the channel and the wind's chop, less in the lee of the shore and the pier, very little in the walled approach, and dying away over the mud. From about force 3 the crests start to break, white on their falling face, leaving lace where they stood. Under Weather, Your own sets any force from 0 to 8 from any of sixteen points; the gusts, the swell and the visibility follow from it.

      Drills

      Come alongside a hammerhead: an empty hammerhead at the end of one of the pontoon arms is outlined in green; let go, come round and lay her alongside it, stopped and parallel, then make fast. Lock out and back in: call the marina, lock out, round the pier head, call again, lock back in and return to your berth. Man overboard: get out into the estuary and above 4 kn; someone goes over the side and drifts with the stream. Turn back, keep them in sight, come up to them slowly into the stream and wind, neutral before they are near the prop, and stop with them alongside the console. O throws someone over at any time.

      The tide

      The tide is worked out on your device from the Avonmouth gauge's harmonic constants (TICON-4, 1961 to 2012), the published Portishead times being Avonmouth's. Fitted to Portishead Marina's 2026 tide table it comes within about 15 cm and 10 minutes of it. Heights are above chart datum. Not for navigation.

      The lock's chamber and gate positions are from OpenStreetMap; its sill and floor levels, the dredged depth just outside the gates, the sluice rates and the stream rates are estimates. Depths elsewhere are the chart's. Not for navigation.
